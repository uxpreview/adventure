# Inklands: Story Foundation, Ironed Out (owner, 2026-09-16)

*The owner's refined direction, verbatim in substance. Treat as the
current foundation, not a finalized plan. Critique 3 (file 07) answers it.*

## 1. The central story
Three years before the game begins, the protagonist was the person who
connected Inklands: repaired roads, carried messages, settled disputes,
maintained crossings, helped farms, organized events. People trusted him
because he always said "I'll handle it." People stopped learning to
maintain things themselves. He began to believe being needed was the same
as being loved.

One disastrous day he had promised several people he would be there: an
urgent practical problem; Joan (simply show up); the dog (return); Morrow
(time together); smaller commitments. When the emergency happened he
chose to fix it, breaking the promises that could not be postponed.
Someone told him: "You always come when there's something to fix. You
never come when there's nothing you can do." That night he left. He
wrote BACK IN AN HOUR. — You. He attempted to leave by sea past the
mark. Pye found him in the water and brought him back. His personal
memories were inaccessible: trauma, exhaustion, collapse; not magic, not
chosen. He wakes on a bench after three years away.

## 2. What the game is about
Surface: a man with no memories returns to a world he helped build.
Deeper: a man who believed he had to be useful to deserve love learns he
can belong without fixing everything. Progression: fixing → understanding
→ sharing responsibility → accepting limits. Sometimes the right action
is to ask, let someone else take it, stay, admit a promise can't be kept,
refuse a harmful task, accept help, leave something unfinished.

## 3. The opening
Bench, sandwich, untied shoe, notebook, the note. Laundry woman: "Oh.
You're back." / "You said you'd only be gone an hour." Three years have
passed. Small awkward recognitions (the bridge, three buttons, a message
from the castle, the dog hesitates, a name almost said, a promise he
doesn't remember). First page: TWELVE THINGS. THEN I CAN GO HOME. He
chooses a temporary name when asked. First task: get the bull home; the
bull is everyone's problem because everyone waited for him.

## 4. The twelve promises
His order preserved; player may postpone or reorder.
- 1–3, the capable helper: Wick (why the old road is closed), Nell (the
  bull), Marget (I owe her). Each with a warning sign.
- 4–6, consequences of his old methods: Val (the hedge kept two
  communities apart), Brack (the lake holds evidence of the night), Holt
  (a temporary fix nobody else could maintain; what he needs is help
  organizing people).
- 7–9, morally complicated: Amos (a date to prove blame), Pye (the eighth
  pot holds something from the sea journey; delivering it exposes a
  secret), Wren (a finish line for a race that became exclusionary). At
  least one crossed out or rewritten by the player.
- 10–12, the people behind the tasks: Joan (already fulfilled; can he sit
  down without repairing something; warm but complicated, she enabled
  him), the man at the crossing (solved by telling the truth about why
  he didn't come back), Dennis (the 8:15 as an old appointment, clock,
  shift or event; meaning open; the railway is not forced back).

## 5. The dog
Cared for by someone else; close to Morrow; new routines; recognizes him
but does not return to its old life. Teaches: love does not require
ownership. Gameplay: notices things, finds paths, rests near him when he
stops trying to accomplish something.

## 6. Morrow
Not the villain: the protagonist's philosophy continuing in someone else.
Admired him; filled the gap; a parallel quest-giver with his own partial,
misunderstood notebook; "Already handled it"; sometimes solves only the
visible problem; possessive of the dog. His fear: that the return makes
what he did meaningless. The arc: neither needs to be indispensable; they
work together. He knows part of the truth, not all.

## 7. The world's connective problem
The railway is removed unless a compelling reason emerges. The old
communication network (bell towers, signal flags, lantern stations,
messenger posts, notice boards, speaking tubes, region-specific methods)
replaces it. He became the system himself. The objective is to help
people communicate directly, never "activate twelve towers."

## 8. The endgame
A gathering he once intended to organize but never completed (feast,
market, festival, race, meeting, reopening). At the start everyone
expects him to arrange it; by the end the regions arrange it themselves.
It succeeds because people communicate directly, Morrow asks for help,
Joan contributes without waiting, the dog moves between people, isolated
regions cooperate, and he accepts imperfection.

## 9. The final confrontation
With his old belief. Morrow tries to run the gathering alone and refuses
help. Bad responses: take over; walk away. The meaningful one: help
Morrow bring other people in. A sequence of delegation, timing, urgency,
listening, accepting an imperfect result.

## 10. The final truth
He left because he could not accept failure, harm, limits, needing help,
being loved without being useful, or the world continuing without him.
The sea was escape from shame. Pye brought him back. Joan (or another)
chose not to tell him everything; morally complicated, not unquestionably
correct. He has the right to know.

## 11. The ending
Return to the bench. Stay (cross out the rest, write I CAME BACK); leave
honestly (no promise to return); pass the notebook to Morrow with a new
first line (ASK FIRST / DON'T DO IT ALONE). Not good/bad endings. Final
image: the bench. "You coming?" "Yeah. In a minute."

## 12. Side quests
Plenty that are not about the central checklist; some fun, some strange,
some touching, some with no deeper meaning. Variety keeps the playful
tone.

## Locked statement
A man returns to a world that remembers him after three years missing,
remembering almost nothing. Carrying twelve unfinished promises, he
learns his failure was not leaving because he did not care, but leaving
because he believed he had to be useful to deserve love. With a dog that
found a new home and a young imitator who inherited his worst habits, he
learns that belonging does not require being indispensable.
