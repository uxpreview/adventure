# Inklands: Critique 2 (2026-09-14)

*Against files 01 to 04. The accepted decisions are taken as the
baseline. This answers the six questions in file 04 and names what is
missing. It argues with the leading direction on the memory loss.*

## 1. The memory loss and the disappearance

**The weak part of the leading direction.** "He chose to forget because
remembering was unbearable" has three problems. It needs a mechanism the
world does not have unless magic is added, which is ruled out. It happens
offstage, so it can only be reported to the player. And it makes his
defining act a retreat inward, which nothing in the world can show and the
player cannot retrace.

**The stronger version: the deliberate act was leaving, not forgetting.**
He wrote "back in an hour" because he could not write "I'm not coming
back." He walked out on the twelve. For a man whose identity was being
needed, quitting is the unforgivable thing, and it is the one sin the whole
world is evidence of. Every overgrown road is him leaving.

The memory loss is the price, not the plan. Concrete event: he tried to
leave by sea, because the sea is the only edge of this world that leads
out, and it is already where a shipful of Vikings has failed to leave for
four hundred years. He rowed out past the mark at night, further than
anyone goes. Pye found him in the water at the tide and brought him back
cold and not remembering. A real injury, not a spell. Pye's line in Act
II: "I found you past the mark. You'd gone further than anyone goes." Pye
never told anyone which way the boat was pointing.

**Who put him on the bench.** He did not return. He was returned. Someone
laid him on the bench under the note he had pinned there himself, and
chose not to tell him what he had done. Recommended: Joan. She loved him
without needing him, and she decided he should get to choose again
without the weight. Scene 7's second note ("YOU DON'T HAVE TO DO ALL
TWELVE") is hers; the "YES, I DO" on the back is his handwriting from an
argument they had before he left. That seeds her in the first ten minutes
without naming her.

**Why this fits the theme.** He wanted to stop being the person everyone
needed, and got it in the worst way: a stranger who is still handed the
list. It also makes the ending the same choice he botched, made again with
knowledge.

**Whether he fully remembers:** no. He learns what he did from evidence
and other people, never from recall. No "and then it all came back."

## 2. Morrow

**As written, no.** A rival who wants everything restored exactly as it was
is a strawman the player always votes against, and file 04 says not to add
characters to make the game larger. That mindset is already in the world:
Wick keeping banners for a king who is not coming, Amos carrying water
nightly to keep a rain system "proved," Marget setting a stall that never
opens.

**As a successor, yes.** Someone who idolised him, picked up his job when
he left, and is doing it his way: alone, badly, refusing help. An
apprentice with his flaw, not a rival with an ideology. Make it the child
from Scene 4 who asks about the bridge, a year older, carrying a copied
notebook with the same twelve lines, stuck on number three. Pressure
without a villain (Morrow is getting hurt and will not stop), a mirror the
player cannot dismiss, and a face for the ending's "share
responsibility."

## 3. Escalating the twelve

Twelve promises that each do six things will feel like twelve of the same
thing. Escalate by changing what a promise IS, in four tiers of three:

- **Errands (1 to 3).** Literal, funny, ten minutes, plainly good to
  finish. What you learn: he was competent and liked.
- **Repairs with a history (4 to 6).** The problem exists because of
  something he did or failed to do (he rang the bell at the wrong hour; he
  promised the hedge and never came). What you learn: people were annoyed
  and he was overcommitted. First choice with a loser.
- **Promises that should not be kept as written (7 to 9).** Doing what the
  notebook says would hurt someone, because the promise was a symptom of
  his control (filling Amos's cistern takes away the one thing he does).
  The first time the player refuses a line in his own hand is the arc.
  This tier holds the evidence of the day he left: the lake, the sea, Pye.
- **The people who loved him (10 to 12).** Not tasks. Joan, where the
  promise was already kept and the only thing to do is sit down. The man
  at the crossing, the one promise he plainly broke. The line, the
  twelfth, the system that would make him unnecessary.

Two authoring rules against repetition: every promise has a different verb
(chase, wait, carry, row, count, cut, climb, listen, race, find, sit,
stay); the notebook order is his order, so skipping ahead is allowed but
later lines need routes the earlier ones open. Let the player physically
cross a line out. The ending needs that mechanic.

## 4. The railway

Keep it. It is the thing he was trying to get running: "twelve things,
then I can go home" was a list of conditions for the line, written by a
man who would not let himself stop before it ran. It is in Scene 6's
vista. It has history: it ran, it stopped, he took its job on foot, and
everyone let him.

Its role at the end is the connection that does not need a person. One
guard: the train that runs at the end must not run the old timetable. The
twelve decide where and when it stops. Otherwise the ending argues for
change while restoring the past.

## 5. The ending

A three-option menu is a quiz. Make the final choice something the player
has been doing all game, at the place the game began: the bench and the
note.

- **Make the note true.** Stay. Cross the list out, sit at Joan's table,
  let the train and Morrow and everyone else carry what he carried.
- **Write an honest one.** Leave, openly, and the world lets him. What
  holds without him is exactly what he built with other people, and
  nothing he did alone.
- **Hand the notebook to Morrow.** The trap. Allowed, and the cost is
  shown quietly: a year on, a kid on a bench.

The name choice folds in: keeping the name he chose on waking is choosing
who he became. The title screen re-letters either way. It is inevitable
because the note in Scene 1 is a lie, and the whole game walks toward the
moment he makes it true or replaces it with the truth.

## 6. Missing or drifted

- **Why was he the only connector?** The physical border rule is gone;
  the social cause needs stating once: he took every job until everyone
  else stopped trying. Marget used to walk to the castle herself.
- **How long was he gone.** Roads do not overgrow in a week. A year. "You
  said an hour." "It's been a year."
- **The wow frame moved** from the ridge to Scene 6. Put the vista on the
  title screen so the first frame still earns a screenshot.
- **The goose** is a direct lift from a game everyone knows. The bull is
  ours and built.
- **Choosing your name** disappeared from the docs. It was the most
  on-brand beat in the pitch. If cut, say why.
- **The player's want in Act I** is "where is home." Keep "twelve things,
  then I can go home" exactly, and let the answer be Joan's.
