# Inklands: Claude Feedback and Current Decisions

## Purpose

This document records which ideas from Claude's feedback are accepted,
revised, or unresolved.

Treat it as a working decision log. Do not reopen settled choices
automatically, but challenge them when there is a strong narrative or
gameplay reason.

## Accepted

### Paper and Ink Are Primarily Visual

The ballpoint-and-watercolor presentation is central to the identity of
*Inklands*, but it should not automatically become literal mythology.

Avoid making the plot depend on ink fading, pages being erased, the
player drawing the world, or a magical Ink Key.

### Deterioration Should Be Concrete

Use practical and social deterioration: - Overgrown roads - Broken
bridges - Closed gates - Unlit lamps - Neglected waterways - Abandoned
railway infrastructure - Missing or inaccurate signs - Failed
deliveries - Forgotten routines - Forgotten names

### Escalate Through the Promises

The twelve promises should begin as small, sometimes ridiculous tasks
and gradually reveal a larger emotional and structural pattern.

A useful progression is: 1. Curiosity 2. Connection 3. Responsibility 4.
Discovery 5. Choice

### Not Every Promise Needs a Tool

Promises may unlock routes, bridges, gates, shortcuts, relationships,
home-base features, traversal options, or new understanding.

### Keep the Stakes Intimate

Avoid generic "save the world" stakes. Focus on people being unable to
get home, broken bridges, missed meetings, forgotten names, failed
messages, and communities becoming isolated.

## Accepted, But Revised

### The Train

The train may remain only if established early as a real part of
Inklands: - A pre-existing railway - A system that once connected
regions - Something the protagonist used or maintained - A route now
neglected or abandoned

It should not appear near the ending as a random symbol or arbitrary
countdown. Its final role is unresolved.

### The Surveyor Idea

Route surveying and maintenance may give the protagonist a practical
past, but "surveyor" should not become his emotional identity.

The stronger framing is connector, route keeper, traveling helper, or
problem-solver. His emotional story is about believing he had to hold
everything together alone.

### World Connections

Roads, bridges, waterways, gates, and railways can physically express
the theme of connection, but should remain grounded rather than becoming
a magical network.

## Current Preferred Direction

He once tried to maintain all twelve connections and promises alone. He
eventually failed, disappeared, and lost or abandoned his memories.

While he was gone, Inklands slowly became less connected: - People
stopped visiting - Routes became unsafe - Messages failed - Shared
routines disappeared - Communities became isolated

He returns and repairs individual problems, gradually learning that he
was not simply a heroic caretaker. He was also controlling, avoidant,
overcommitted, and unwilling to let others help.

## Major Unresolved Questions

### 1. What Caused the Memory Loss?

This is the most important unresolved issue.

The leading possibility is that he deliberately or semi-deliberately
escaped, abandoned, or accepted the loss of his memories because
remembering had become unbearable.

We need a specific event: - What happened on the day he disappeared? -
What promise did he fail? - Who was hurt? - What choice did he make? -
Why did forgetting seem like the only way forward? - Was the loss
self-protection, punishment, manipulation, sacrifice, or a
combination? - Why did he return now?

### 2. Is Morrow Necessary?

Morrow could be a person who took over after the protagonist left and
believes Inklands must be restored exactly as it was.

That would make Morrow a reflection of the protagonist's old mindset
rather than a purely evil villain.

However, the story may work better without a conventional antagonist if
the main conflict is between the protagonist, his past choices, and the
consequences of his absence.

Morrow should remain only if he creates meaningful pressure and
emotional contrast.

### 3. Should the Railway Drive the Ending?

The railway is a strong possible symbol because it literally connects
regions. It must be seeded early and have emotional history.

Possible functions: - A route he once maintained - The site of an
important event - A way for communities to reconnect - A final image of
people traveling without needing him to guide them

### 4. What Is the Final Choice?

The ending should not be "save the world" versus "destroy the world."

It should concern: - Restoring everything exactly as it was versus
accepting change - Solving every problem personally versus trusting
others - Choosing an identity based on relationships rather than
usefulness

## Questions for the Next Critique

Please focus on: 1. A concrete and emotionally powerful cause for the
memory loss 2. The event that caused his disappearance 3. Escalating the
twelve promises without repetition 4. Whether Morrow is necessary 5.
Whether the railway belongs in the story 6. An ending that feels
emotionally inevitable 7. Any major missing element in the story or
world

## Collaboration Standard

We want honest criticism, not automatic agreement.

If an idea is weak: - Explain what is weak - Identify the narrative
problem - Offer a stronger alternative - Explain trade-offs

Do not add lore, mechanics, characters, or regions merely to make the
game larger. Every addition should strengthen the central emotional
story.
