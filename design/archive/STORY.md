# STORY — THE 8:15

*Locked by the owner, 2026-08-29. This is the bible: the premise, the
shape, the acts, the cast and the rules of the fiction. It is binding on
every session from Session 7 on, the way `QUALITY-BAR.md` is binding on
how things look. `design/QUESTS.md` is its companion and holds the
content architecture — what a quest is in a game with no quest log.*

*A future session maps out all the stories in detail. This one fixes
what they are stories ABOUT, so that work has a spine to hang on.*

---

## 0. THE RULE THAT COMES FIRST

**The medium is the STYLE. It is never the SUBJECT.**

No story about the paper, the pen, the drawing, or the person who drew
it. Every candidate this project carried before 2026-08-29 was one of
those, and three sessions of design drifted on it before the owner
caught it. The ballpoint, the washes, the hand-lettering and the
sheet's terrain vocabulary all stay — that is **craft**, and craft is
where the medium belongs. Nothing in this file may reach for it.

If a future session finds itself writing a note about "the page" or "the
pen" or "whoever drew you", it has drifted. Stop and reread this line.

---

## 1. THE PREMISE

In the far south-east corner of the world there is a stop with a
timetable on it.

> *the timetable says the 8:15 is coming. the 8:15 is drawn nowhere on
> this sheet. everyone waiting knows both of these things and has made
> their peace.*

That line has been in the game since Session 1. It is the best sentence
anybody has written for this project and it is now the spine.

Because **every land in this world is waiting for something**, in its
own century's idiom, and none of them will tell you so directly.
Greyweather's banners are still up for a king who rode out and did not
come back, and somebody is still changing them. Brim's market has been
"next week" for a long time: the stalls are set, the bunting is up, and
the belfry clock's two hands disagree about the hour. Maple Court leaves
its porch lights on. The Harrow Downs waits for the harvest, which is
the only wait in the world that has ever been answered.

And **nobody can leave their own land.** The people of this world are
of their land and do not cross its border — they will walk beside you
to the edge of it and stop dead (WORLD-SYSTEMS §5, the co-walker). They
have therefore never compared notes. Each of them is certain that the
thing they are waiting for is coming from *somewhere else*.

**You are the only thing in the world that crosses.** So sooner or
later, every land decides you must be the one they have been waiting
for. You are not. But you are the only messenger this world has ever
had.

## 2. THE SHAPE — twelve waits, one wait

*Owner reference, 2026-08-29: Calvino's* **Invisible Cities** *(1972).
Scoped and credited, with everything else this project learns from, in
`design/INSPIRATION.md`.*

Marco Polo describes fifty-five cities to Kublai Khan. They are sorted
into eleven categories of five — Cities & Memory, Cities & Desire,
Thin Cities, Cities & the Dead — and each description is short, exact
and slightly fabular. The turn, when it comes, is that they are all
Venice.

That is our structure exactly, and it is worth stealing on purpose:

- **twelve lands = twelve waits**, each described in its own register,
  each self-contained, each completable in any order;
- **four categories of three**, so twelve does not read as a list;
- **a frame**: Polo carries the cities to a Khan who cannot go and see
  them. Our walker carries the lands to each other, because they cannot
  go and see each other;
- **and the turn: they are all the same wait.**

It also fixes the VOICE for a land's own story: short, precise,
unhurried, and never explaining the joke. Calvino never once tells you
the cities are Venice.

## 3. THE TWELVE WAITS

| land | waits for | which makes it believe | category |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CASTLE GREYWEATHER** | a king who rode out and did not come back; the banners are still up and somebody is still changing them | that loyalty is a thing you go on doing after it stops making sense | Memory |
| **THE KINGDOM OF BRIM** | market day, which has been next week for a while: stalls set, bunting up, the clock's hands disagreeing | that if you keep the ritual exactly, the thing comes back | Memory |
| **MAPLE COURT** | the people who left. The porch lights are on, and the swing still moves | that leaving is temporary | Memory |
| **SPLITROCK CANYON** | the river that cut it, which is now somebody else's river | that the world used to be more generous and may be again | Weather |
| **THE BLEACH FLATS** | rain | that the answer is elsewhere, and that elsewhere owes you | Weather |
| **LONGSHORE** | the tide, which comes twice a day and is the most reliable thing anybody here knows | that waiting is just a shape the day has | Weather |
| **THE PENWOOD** | whatever is in the tarn. Nobody goes near it and nobody will say why | that some things should be waited out rather than met | The Unseen |
| **THE WIDE BLUE** | the end of a race that has been sailed round the same mark since before anyone was counting | that the point was never the finish | The Unseen |
| **THE COMMON** | whoever comes down the road. Four names on the signpost and one of them is not a place | nothing yet — which is why you start here | The Unseen |
| **THE HARROW DOWNS** | the harvest | that waiting is just work with a date on it. **The only land whose wait is answered, and the story's counterweight** | Work |
| **GREYLINE CITY** | nothing. Everybody is going somewhere and nobody arrives | that waiting is for people who have not made it | Work |
| **THE CUBICLE MILE** | the 8:15 | that a timetable is a promise, and a promise is enough | Work |

**What a land is waiting for is what it believes**, which hands all
twelve regions an ideology at no extra authoring cost — the Fallout
region thesis (WORLD-SYSTEMS §10) for free.

### The four categories

- **WAITS & MEMORY** — waiting for something that has already gone.
  Warm, and the saddest of the four. The old world.
- **WAITS & WEATHER** — waiting on something indifferent that was never
  coming *for* you. The dry lands and the coast.
- **WAITS & THE UNSEEN** — waiting for something nobody has ever seen,
  including the thing at the crossroads where you begin.
- **WAITS & WORK** — waiting as an economy: scheduled, monetised, or
  denied outright. The modern south-east.

## 4. THE ACTS

### ACT I — *You are the one they were waiting for. You are not.*

You wake at the Common's crossroads. You walk, because it is the only
verb you have. In the first lands you meet, people react to you as
though you were expected — and it is a misunderstanding every time.

It is funny first and then it is not. By the end of Act I the player
has learned three facts, none of them stated: **everybody here is
waiting**, **nobody here can leave**, and **you can**.

### ACT II — *The twelve waits.*

Open. Any order. Each land's wait is a self-contained arc with its own
people, its own idiom and its own turn — this is the bulk of the game
and where the hours live.

What accumulates is not items. It is **knowledge**: what each land
waits for, why, and — the part that starts to matter — what its
neighbours would think of it if they knew. Twelve answers in twelve
registers start to rhyme.

**The Act II hinge is already in the built world.** The Common's
crossroads signpost has four names on it and one of them is not a
place. It is a **time**.

### ACT III — *The line.*

You reach THE CUBICLE MILE, the newest and thinnest corner of the
world, and find the timetable and no track.

And then the thing the player has been walking over for fifteen hours
without seeing it: **the road is the line.** The king's road leaves
Greyweather's gate, comes down through Brim, crosses the Common, runs
up Maple Court as main street and ends, as the commuter spur, in a car
park. Twelve names, one road, castle to car park, dead straight in
intent if not in fact. It was **surveyed as a railway** and built as a
road by people who were waiting for it to become the other thing.

Everybody in the world lives on it. Nobody has ever walked all of it.

### ACT IV — *The 8:15.*

It comes.

WORLD-SYSTEMS §4 already made the 8:15's arrival the reward for
finishing the office park, and made a railway "a ruled line across the
world, in the one quadrant drawn with a straightedge". That is the
ending of the game, not a mount unlock: a train comes the whole length
of the world, once, for the first time.

## 5. THE TURN

They were all waiting for **each other**.

Twelve lands on one road; nobody able to cross; every one of them
certain that what was coming was coming from somewhere else. The thing
coming from somewhere else was the other eleven, and the only reason
nobody knew is that nobody could go and look.

That is the Calvino move and it is the whole story in one sentence. It
is never said aloud by anybody, ever.

## 6. THE ENDING — *a proposal, to be settled when the stories are mapped*

The 8:15 arrives, and it can do the one thing nobody in this world has
ever been able to do: carry a person out of their own land. Everyone is
on it, because you told them.

Everyone except **Joan Harrow**, who is not on the platform, because
her harvest came in and she never needed a train.

The last thing the player does is decide whether to get on.

*(Flagged honestly: this needs to land or the whole game is a shrug,
and it is the one beat that cannot be fixed later by polish. It is a
proposal, not law, and the session that maps the stories owns it.)*

## 7. THE CAST

**No faces** (QUALITY-BAR §3). So a person here is exactly four things:
a **posture**, a **place**, a **routine**, and a **name**. Nothing else
about them changes, ever — and that is enough, because it is enough in
RDR2 and Fallout too.

**The naming convention IS the map.** Names get plainer and more modern
as you walk east-by-south, because the geography argues time
(DIRECTION.md). Nobody comments on this either.

| who | where | posture, place, routine |
|---|---|---|
| **WICK** | Castle Greyweather | changes the banners on the avenue, every day, alone. Nobody has told him the king is not coming back. He knows |
| **MARGET** | Brim, the square | sets the stall out at dawn, lays the cloth, does not open, packs it away at dusk |
| **NELL** | The Common, the field gate | leans on the gate watching the road. She is already in the game — the doodle-folk figure at the long fence. She has been watching a long time |
| **BRACK** | The Penwood | will not go within forty units of the tarn, and moves so that he never has his back to it |
| **PYE** | Longshore, the cove | rows out at the tide, comes back at the tide, keeps pots that catch nothing much |
| **JOAN HARROW** | The Harrow Downs | works. The only person in the world waiting for something that actually arrives, and therefore the only one who is not really waiting. **The story's counterweight and its conscience** |
| **THE MAN AT THE JUNCTION** | Greyline City | stands still in the one land where standing still is shameful, waiting for somebody. Everyone walks around him. He has no name yet and that is the point |
| **DENNIS** | The Cubicle Mile | the desk plate says D. HALL. Has the timetable memorised. Has never once suggested the train is not coming, and would be embarrassed for you if you did |

**And from 2026-09-01 the cast is wider** (`THE-FUN-PASS.md` §10, all
kept by the owner): the Vikings on the Holdfast, the aliens in the Pale,
the surfers at the Cut, the barista at Greyline's junction, the design
studio in the Cubicle Mile's atrium, the monsters that are never seen
whole, and animals in every land. None of them gets a wait; the twelve
stay twelve. None of them crosses a border. None of them explains
anything.

Plus the standing device from WORLD-SYSTEMS §5: **the co-walker** —
somebody who falls in beside you for a stretch and stops dead at their
land's border. That mechanic is now a fact about the world rather than
a nice touch, and it is how the player learns Act I's second fact
without being told.

## 8. RULES OF THE FICTION

Binding on anybody writing content for this world.

1. **Nobody crosses a border but you.** This is the engine of the whole
   story. Mounts are the player's alone (WORLD-SYSTEMS §4); no
   inhabitant ever uses one to leave.
2. **The world never explains itself.** Twelve centuries coexist on one
   landmass and nobody finds it odd. Played straight it produces both
   the comedy and the melancholy; explained, it produces neither.
   *(Amended 2026-09-01, `THE-FUN-PASS.md` §2.5: absolute for the
   anachronism, the turn and the ending. Relaxed for wayfinding —
   people may point, signs may say what they are for, a note may
   carry an instruction when the instruction is the invitation.)*
3. **The humour is deadpan and structural**, never a gag. A
   civilisation sincerely organised around a train with no track is
   funny in proportion to how straight everyone plays it. Nobody in
   this world is doing a bit.
4. **Nothing is urgent. There is no villain.** The opposition is the
   waiting, and the waiting is nobody's fault. If a session finds
   itself writing an antagonist, it has taken a wrong turn.
   *(Amended 2026-09-01, `THE-FUN-PASS.md` §2.3: still no villain, no
   combat, no fail state, no timer. **Local stakes are allowed** —
   weather, the dark, water, a bull, a thing on a beach that chases
   you off it, a sound in a wood that stops. Fear never needs an
   enemy and never kills you.)*
5. **Nobody says the turn.** Not the narrator, not a note, not a
   character, not the ending. The player assembles it or they do not.
6. **A land's own story is told in its own century's register.**
   Greyweather does not talk like the office park. This is most of the
   texture and it is free.
7. **The wry narrator stays; the winking stops.** The note voice is
   good and it is the house voice. What it must stop doing is pointing
   at the medium — *"the tallest drawing on the sheet"*, *"a drawing
   bragging about being a drawing"*. **About a third of the thirty-five
   existing notes need this pass**, Session 5's coast notes worst of
   all.
