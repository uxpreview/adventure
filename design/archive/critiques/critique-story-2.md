# Critique — THE STORY GATE, first run (2026-08-30)

*Owner, 2026-08-30: "every gate this project has ever run has judged
PICTURES. THE LINE was mapped in one session and read by nobody."
`QUALITY-BAR` §2 names the gate and its five questions. It is cheap, it
needs no build, and the Session 8 brief allowed it to run beside a score
session **if there was room at the end**. There was. This is it.*

**Run as an adversarial read, not a review** — a critic trying to find
the shrug, per §2. Sources read cold: `THE-LINE.md` end to end,
`THE-WAITS.md` §13 (the twelve contributions), and `STORY.md` §§5–8.

**And the one caveat that governs everything below, stated first: this
is a read of DOCUMENTS.** Not one beat of Act I, Act III or Act IV is
built. Prose is not a game and a table is not a frame, so this gate is
the cheapest possible time to find these things and simultaneously the
least reliable place to find them. Nothing here re-opens `THE-LINE.md`
§5, which is binding: the findings are authoring notes for the sessions
that build Acts I, III and IV.

**Verdict: NOT YET.** Two mandatory, three recommended. The spine is
sound and the ending is not a shrug — but two of Act I's three facts
hang on a single optional beat, and the ending's DEFAULT witness sees
the weakest version of it.

---

## Q1 · Does Act I teach its three facts without stating them?

*everybody is waiting · nobody can leave · you can*

**Fact one lands.** Nell straightening, Marget's laid cloth with nothing
on it, Wick's rolled banner on an empty avenue — three instances in
three registers, none of them narrated. This is the part of Act I that
works, and I.5's *she looks up when you come into the square, once. She
does not do it twice* is the best line in the file.

**Facts two and three do not have three teachers between them. They
have one.**

I.3 is the only beat in Act I where a person fails to cross a border:
Nell falls in, walks the road, stops dead. Fact three — *you can* — has
**no beat of its own anywhere in the table**; it exists entirely as the
negative space of I.3, the thing the player does one second later
without being told it was remarkable. That is elegant and it is also
load-bearing to a degree the table does not acknowledge.

And I.3 fires on **"leaving north with Nell met."** So:

- a player who walks EAST from the crossroads — toward the Downs, which
  is a perfectly ordinary first choice on a signpost with four names on
  it — meets no co-walker;
- a player who does not walk within sight of the long fence never meets
  Nell at all;
- I.7 ("the second time it happens") **cannot fire until the first one
  has**, so the repetition the design relies on to turn an event into a
  rule inherits the same single point of failure.

Two of the three facts, and the entire premise of the walker, are
downstream of one optional, directional beat.

> ### MANDATORY 1 — the co-walker must not depend on which way the
> player leaves.
>
> Make it a rule of the world rather than a scripted beat: **anybody the
> walker is travelling near stops at their own border**, on any road out
> of any land. Then whichever of the four names on the signpost the
> player picks, somebody walks with them and somebody stops, and I.7's
> second instance is a matter of time rather than of route.
> It is also cheaper than the scripted version and truer to `STORY.md`
> §8 rule 1, which is a law and not a set piece.

**A second, smaller thing, and this project has been bitten by it
before.** I.2 asks the player to read *straighten → settle* on a
standee with no face, at the shipping camera, and to attribute both to
their own approach. Session 7's own gotcha: *a silent system still has
to be visible — the map's registers needed THREE signals, not one,
because the difference did not survive the scale.* Nell's straighten is
currently one signal. Whichever session builds her should assume it
needs two or three (posture, and the gate she is leaning on, and where
her feet point), and should shoot it before believing it.

## Q2 · Does the turn survive not being said?

*Read `THE-WAITS.md` §13 cold. Do you assemble* they were all waiting
for each other?

**Not quite. What a cold reader assembles from those twelve lines is
that everything anybody needs is somewhere else ON THIS SHEET, in
somebody else's land.** That is the rhyme `THE-LINE.md` §2 rule 2
names, and it lands hard — Splitrock's river running forty units away
in another land, the Flats' water always coming from somewhere on this
page, Maple Court's hedge. Six of them and the pattern is unmistakable.

But *waiting for each other* is a **reciprocal** claim and the table is
mostly one-way. Counted honestly, four of the twelve carry reciprocity:
Greyweather (*two lands were once served by one trade*), Splitrock and
the Flats as a matched pair, and the Cubicle Mile (*the twelve are on
it, in order*). The other eight say **my answer is over there** and not
**and I am theirs.**

The distance between *the thing I need is next door* and *we are each
other's answer* is small in a world where nobody can cross — and it is
also the whole thesis. On this evidence a reader gets to the first and
has to be generous to reach the second.

> ### RECOMMENDED — two more of the twelve should point BOTH ways.
>
> The cheapest candidates are the pairs that already face each other:
> LONGSHORE's *there is a thing out there and it has a name* and THE
> WIDE BLUE's *a circle can be a line if somebody puts down a second
> mark* are one relationship written from one side twice. If the second
> mark is Pye's — if the Wide Blue's answer is visibly the thing
> Longshore was missing and the sea can see the shore needing it — the
> pair becomes reciprocal for no new content at all.
>
> This may already be true in the waits' full text; §13 is a summary.
> But **§13 is the artifact this gate was told to read cold**, and a
> summary that under-sells the thesis will under-sell it to the next
> author too.

## Q3 · Does the ending land, or is it a shrug?

**It is not a shrug, and the three changes `THE-LINE.md` §4.5 made are
all right.** Removing the final choice is correct — a binary at the end
of a game with no fail states is a quiz, and the walker has never
wanted anything. Twelve stops instead of one platform is not a
compromise, it is better: the train going the length of the world past
everybody's door is a stronger image than a crowd, and it is the only
version that does not retract rule 1 in the last minute.

**The defect is not in the ending. It is in who sees it.**

The train stops twelve times. The player is standing in exactly one
place. Unless they ride, **their whole ending is one stop** — and
nothing anywhere guarantees that the stop they happen to be standing at
is one of the lands whose wait they answered. The default experience of
a player who qualifies while standing in a land they did nothing for
is: *a train arrives, the doors open on an empty platform, it waits
half a minute, it leaves.*

That is the exact shrug `STORY.md` §6 was afraid of, arrived at from a
direction §6 did not consider. §4.4 answers it — get on — but getting
on is optional and the game may not say so twice.

> ### MANDATORY 2 — the 8:15 must arrive already carrying people.
>
> It comes down the line **from the north** and stops **in order**
> (§4.2), so this costs nothing and changes no clause of §5: by the
> time it reaches any land, it is already carrying whoever got on at
> the lands above it, and they are **visible through the windows**.
> Then the player at an empty platform in the eleventh land still sees
> the consequence of fifteen hours go past at eye level, and the
> invitation to get on is made by the carriage rather than by a prompt.
>
> A player standing at the first stop — Greyweather — sees an empty
> train, which is correct: they are at the top of the line and nothing
> has happened yet.

## Q4 · Is Act III a reveal or an anticlimax?

**The argument in §3.2 is sound and I will not argue with it.** Two
hundred units of dead straight empty road going into haze, and *you
cannot see where it ends, you can see that it does not stop*, is a
better frame than one that answers the question. The road stopping
sixteen units short of the rim since Session 1 and being given a reason
retroactively is the best piece of design archaeology in these files.

Two flags, neither fatal.

**One: Act III has no event in it.** Its three deliveries are an
absence (no track), a view (the rim), and a change to a screen (the
map). The strongest of the three is the map — and it lands *the next
time the player happens to open it*, untriggered and untimed, in a
game that will not prompt them. That is almost certainly fine, because
the map is the record and players open it constantly; but it is the
one beat in the game whose delivery is entirely at the player's
discretion, and it should be understood that way rather than assumed.

**Two, and it is for Session 11: §3.2's claim has never been
photographed.** Every other protected composition in this project
earned a WOWED on a real frame. The rim is a composition that has been
*argued* — beautifully — and does not exist. It is the single riskiest
un-shot frame in the game, and it should be the FIRST thing Session 11
shoots, not the last. If two hundred units of empty road into haze
reads as an empty page rather than as a survey line, the entire third
act needs re-planning, and that is much cheaper to learn on day one of
that session than on its last afternoon.

## Q5 · Does it work if the player does the acts out of order?

**Mostly yes, and the mechanism is the reason.** A wait resolves when
the player, *holding the right knowledge*, arrives at the right place —
so a player who reaches the Cubicle Mile in the first ten minutes finds
a stop, a timetable and no track, and none of it resolves into
anything. The design does not need a lock because knowledge is the
lock. That is the system working exactly as `QUESTS.md` Tier 0 claims.

**But one of the twelve pieces is not like the others.** The Cubicle
Mile's contribution is *there is a list, and the twelve are on it, in
order* — which is not a detail that rhymes with eleven other details.
**It is the assembly instructions.** A player who reads that one first
is handed the shape of the answer and spends the remaining eleven
lands confirming a thesis instead of building one, which is precisely
the failure mode §2 rule 2 exists to prevent (*reading two is a
coincidence, reading six is the story, and the player does the
reading*).

> ### RECOMMENDED — the list should mean nothing until some of the names
> on it do.
>
> Not a gate and not a lock: an in-fiction condition of the kind this
> game already uses. **A timetable is a list of place-names**, and a
> player who has stood in two lands recognises two of them and a player
> who has stood in nine recognises nine. Let the Cubicle Mile's wait
> resolve on holding several lands' NAMES — which the map already
> tracks in three registers, and which the player earns simply by
> having been places. Early, it is a list of words. Late, it is the
> order of the world.

## What this gate did not do

It did not read the twelve waits in full — §13's summary table is what
`QUALITY-BAR` §2 names and what a cold read gets. It did not judge
prose quality; Session 7's voice pass owns that and did it properly. And
it did not touch the ending: `THE-LINE.md` §5 is binding, nothing above
changes a clause of it, and MANDATORY 2 is an authoring note about what
is visible from a platform rather than a change to what happens.

**This critic is also a proposal**, exactly as the STORY EDITOR was
(`critique-story-1.md` §3). It has now run once and is logged. Whether
a story gate becomes standing — and whether NOT YET here means the
acts are blocked or merely annotated — is the owner's call, because
unlike the art director it is judging something that does not exist
yet.
