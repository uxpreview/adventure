# Critique — THE STORY GATE, fourth run: the whole thing, as built (2026-09-06)

*Session 22. `THE-FUN-PASS` §14 row 22 asked for this: **the story
gate, run to WOWED**, on the whole game, on top of what the fun pass
built. `critique-story-1` proposed the gate, `-2` ran it cold on
documents and returned NOT YET with two mandatory and two recommended
findings, `-3` re-ran it on Act I as built and passed it. This is the
first read of all four acts on a BUILD. Sources: the twelve waits with
both doors taken on the harness (`check-verbs` §11 and §12), the two
new fresh-page runs (§13, §14), the notes as re-keyed this session
(read in full in their lands' keys), `THE-LINE.md` §5, `THE-STRANGERS`

**Run as an adversarial read**, with the five questions `QUALITY-BAR`
§2 names, and one more this session earned: does a thing the walker
WEARS break the fiction?

**Verdict: PASSED, NOT WOWED.** Every mandatory finding on the record
is closed in the source. Two of the twelve waits still land in a key
that is not their category's, and the ending's larger permanence is a
decision nobody has made. WOWED is a verdict on a thing that has been
played, and this gate reads; the play gate has not come back on six
sheets. What follows is what the read can say.

## Q1 · Do the four categories read as four kinds of hour?

`THE-FUN-PASS` §4 re-keyed the twelve into MEMORY (emotional), WEATHER
(awe), THE UNSEEN (frightening) and WORK (funny, the Downs excepted).
The read went land by land, note by note, asking one question of each:
*if this were the only note you read in this land, which key would you
say it was in?*

**MEMORY holds.** Brim's belfry under the clock set to eight — *one
stall in the square has not opened, and lays its cloth at dawn, and
folds it at dusk, at an hour nobody else keeps any more* — is the
emotional key exactly: a return visit that pays off routine, and a cost
written as a routine. Greyweather's *whoever lives here is never home,
or is the wind* and Maple Court's *it is always almost dinnertime
here* are the same hour. The crown's clause on the plinth — *his head
is bare. nobody has mentioned it.* — is in key because it adds a loss
and does not add a joke.

**WEATHER holds, after two rewrites.** Splitrock's *you stand under it.
it holds* and the long water's *that is the loudest thing in it* were
already awe. THE MOUTH (*the page is only scratched here*) and WHERE
THE ROAD STOPS (*the next sheet down*) were winks at the paper — rule 7
of `STORY.md` §8, the medium as subject — and awe cannot survive a
wink; both are rewritten to be about the ground and the edge. THE
PALE's *on the sheet* went the same way.

**THE UNSEEN holds, after three rewrites, and one land is borderline.**
THE WOOD ROAD ended on *a great deal more polite*, which is the funny
key in the frightening land; it ends now on *something in here ticks.
it stops when you stop*, which is the pine-tick the land already plays
and the first time a note has pointed at it. THE TARN's *black as the
good ink* (four variants) was the medium again; it is *black, and it
does not move when the wind does*. RIVERBEND on the Common was a
cursive joke and is now a thing the river is carrying that you cannot
see. **The borderline is THE WIDE BLUE**: its frightening content is
the long water, the thing that surfaces at dusk, and the sea nobody
swims in, and its NOTES are mostly comedy — the mark, the longship, the
Vikings, and now the helm. The category is right (the sea is the
unseen), the tone of what you read there is the cast's, not the
land's. Recorded below as RECOMMENDED 1.

**WORK holds, and the Downs is the counterweight it was meant to be.**
The muster point, the barrier's kettle, the overflow's sheep, the
cart's cups, the junction *a little tired about it*: every one is
Vault-Tec played straight. The lanyard that says VISITOR is the
sprint's own joke told a fourth time and it lands because nobody at
the sprint has ever had one. The Downs' notes are warm and not funny;
the scarecrow keeps the one joke it had, and the session declined to
take its hat for exactly this reason.

## Q2 · Does the story still never say the turn?

Read adversarially, with the new material: the crown, the lanyard, the
helm, the hat, Odd's mark, the timetable's names.

**Nothing new says it.** The closest approach is the stop's note once
six names are held — *they are places, and you have stood in most of
them, and they are in the order they are in* — and that is
`critique-story-2` RECOMMENDED 2 built as written: the list means
nothing until the names do, and when they do, the note says what the
board says and not what it means. *The order they are in* is a fact
about a timetable. It is not *the same wait*.

**Odd's mark is the tier's best example of not saying.** The whole
stranger is a man who wants a number; the resolution is a mark with
nothing written beside it, and the note reads *nothing written beside
it* and stops. `check-verbs` §14 asserts the note never contains *how
deep* or *deeper than*. That is the rule enforced by a tool, which is
where this project puts rules it means.

## Q3 · Do the doors still have no right answer, with the worn things in?

This is the question the session's own material raises. A cosmetic is,
in every game the owner named, a REWARD, and a reward for a door is a
grade on the door.

**They are not rewards, on three counts.** First, none is gated on
WHICH door: the crown is offered after either door at the plinth; the
lanyard after either at the board; the helm after the fleet's second
door, which is a door the design already made cost the most. Second,
each one is a COST to its land, drawn permanently — the bare king, the
bare bow man, the bare easel, the hat that never runs — the same shape
as a door's cost, not a door's prize. Third, nothing counts them, lists
them or names a set; the button says what is on and goes round, and it
is not on the page until the first is taken. The one place the read
stalls: the helm arrives only after `door:the-fleet-finished` and not
after `door:the-second-mark`, so a walker who set the mark and did not
call the finish never gets it. That is a consequence, not a grade — the
fleet only stops rowing under the finish, and a helm only comes ashore
from a crew with nothing to do — but it is the one wearable that reads
as the wages of one door. Recorded below as RECOMMENDED 2.

## Q4 · Can a reader who has played everything say what the world believes?

`critique-story-3` could answer for Act I. On the whole: **yes, and
the doors are why.** Twelve waits, each with a cost on either side, and
an ending that reads which side; a walker who has been through all
twelve has been asked twelve times what a wait is for and has never
once been told. The train comes for seven decisions, either way. The
platforms show what was decided and not whether it was right. A reader
who assembles *they were all the same wait* (`STORY.md` §5) does it
from twelve costs, which is a stronger assembly than twelve rewards
would have been.

**What weakens it:** the larger departure-permanence. Today the people
who got on the train are back where they stand the next morning. The
ending says the wait ended for them; the world says it did not. This
is `PROMPT.md` §2's owner decision and the gate cannot make it, but the
gate can say that until it is made the ending is a scene and not a
consequence, and every door's cost is more permanent than the ending's.
That is the single thing between PASSED and WOWED that a session could
build.

## Q5 · Is the fright a fright?

The Penwood's wood road now points at the tick, the tarn does not move
when the wind does, and the deep pines still work somewhere you cannot
tell which way. The Common's river bend carries something you cannot
see. The Pale at night is unchanged and it is the best frightening
content in the game. **The Wide Blue is where the fright is thinnest**
(Q1's borderline), and the helm on the foreshore — a thing thrown from
a ship that cannot land, further than they have ever reached — is the
first note there that is frightening for a sentence before it is funny.
That is the right order for that cast, and it is one sentence.

---

> ### RECOMMENDED 1 — THE WIDE BLUE's own notes should be in its key,
> and the cast's can stay in theirs.
>
> THE SHALLOWS, THE SANDBAR, THE MARK and THE SEAWARD FACE are the
> land's; the longship's and the punt's are the cast's. The land's four
> read as awe and deadpan. One of them — the shallows, whose subject is
> already *the sea is not somewhere a person goes* — should be
> frightening on the page: what the blue does past the knee, said
> plainly. One note, one land, the next session that opens the coast.

> ### RECOMMENDED 2 — the helm should come ashore under either of
> Wren's doors, or the reason it does not should be visible.
>
> Under the second mark alone the fleet still races and the crew still
> row, so nothing is thrown; the read accepts that. But a player who
> took the mark will never know a helm was possible, and a cost they
> never saw is not a cost. Either the bow man throws it at the mark's
> first evening race too (a different reason, the same helm), or the
> longship's note under the mark door says what he did not do. The
> second is one clause.

> ### RECORDED, NOT A FINDING — the larger departure-permanence.
>
> Q4 says why. It is the owner's, in `PROMPT.md` §2 since Session 21,
> and this gate's WOWED waits on it as much as on the play gate.
